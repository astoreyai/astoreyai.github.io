Verification Engineering for Agentic AI: A Research Agenda

I am Aaron W. Storey, an independent AI research engineer at Kymera Systems LLC and a PhD Candidate in Computer Science at Clarkson University. This note states the research agenda I plan to pursue on verification engineering for agentic AI. It is an agenda, not a set of results. No experiment that measures how verifiers err together has run yet, and nothing below should be read as a finding.

The problem

Agent pipelines often promote a piece of work when a panel of checkers agrees. The checkers may be LLM judges, generated tests, or rule-based validators. The promotion rule assumes the checkers err independently. Usually they do not. Checkers built from the same model family, prompts, retrieval corpora, or tools share blind spots, so their errors correlate. When that happens, agreement can say little about correctness. I call this circular confirmation: the panel agrees because its members share structure, not because the answer is right.

Adding more members of the same kind does not fix this. If every member fails on the same claims, a larger panel agrees more often on the same wrong answers, and the agreement rate tells the operator nothing new. This follows from what a shared cause is. It is an argument, not a measured result.

The problem sits close to scalable oversight and AI control. Debate (Irving, Christiano, and Amodei, 2018), AI control protocols (Greenblatt et al., 2024), LLM-as-judge evaluation (Zheng et al., 2023), and Constitutional AI (Bai et al., 2022) all rely on model judgment checking model output. My question is what those checks are worth when the checkers are not independent.

The claim, narrowed

My working claim is a claim about composition. Below a threshold number of human-adjudicated episodes, the error correlations among the members of a panel cannot be estimated jointly to a decision-relevant precision. At or above that threshold, a validated estimator can estimate them and carry its uncertainty into a statement about the panel as a whole. Panel-confidence claims should therefore carry a budget certificate that states what their calibration cost. A system below the threshold should abstain from confidence claims it cannot afford.

The parts that matter are one adjudication budget shared across all pairs of a panel, stratification by truth (correlation conditional on the true label, not marginal agreement), and uncertainty carried through to the panel level. The per-pair sample size result for a single agreement table is prior art. Bonett and Price (2007) published a confidence interval and sample size formula for a generalized Yule coefficient. I reproduced their closed-form results and use them as a design aid, not as my contribution.

I narrowed the claim this summer after checking it against my own proofs. My earlier version held that a measured effective-independence count determines decision-relevant precision. A witness in my working notes (Theorem 8.3) shows two five-verifier panels with identical pairwise error correlations and identical effective-independence values, whose unanimous-verdict errors differ by a factor of 3.55. That count is one input, not the answer. One candidate for the statistic beyond it is channel-level dependence: which named inputs the members share, established by intervening on them one at a time.

Where things stand

  • No experiment measuring verifier error correlation has run.
  • One small pricing pilot has run: 25 FEVER claims judged by five locally hosted open-weight verifiers. It timed human adjudication and did not estimate correlation. Human adjudication is not yet collected, so I do not report its summary statistics.
  • GroundBench, a benchmark, is partly built: three of fourteen task families. Its held-out split is sealed. The model-verdict stage is blocked on API credentials, so no verifier has produced a verdict on it. Verifiers from separate vendors cannot run for the same reason.
  • Earlier design-stage simulations found that the compound decision rule I first registered reaches only about 0.50 power at its own threshold. That result concerns the rule, not data. The rule is under revision.

The plan

The agenda has three aims.

  1. Estimate the joint error law of a verifier panel on real labeled claims, using one shared adjudication budget, with pairwise correlations stratified by truth.
  2. Make panel confidence auditable. Each reported panel verdict carries a certificate that states the budget spent and the precision it bought. A panel below the threshold abstains.
  3. Test whether coverage is real. Verifier stacks are built with known shared blind spots. A coverage metric counts only if it detects the planted blind spots it should detect.

Validation runs in a fixed order. The estimator is checked against known truth first (in controlled simulations, labeled as such), then against the constructed GroundBench stacks. No claim about a deployed system will be made until both checks pass.

First experiment. I will adjudicate the 25 pilot episodes blind to gold labels and panel verdicts, then estimate the truth-stratified pairwise error correlations among the five verifiers with bootstrap intervals. At this size the intervals will be wide. The experiment tests the pipeline end to end, prices adjudication, and shows what 25 episodes can and cannot support. I am not stating an expected result.

How this could fail

  • The Bonett and Price (2007) article body has not been read, because publisher access was blocked. If it contains a treatment of several tables with a shared sample, the shared-budget part of my claim weakens, and I would reopen it.
  • The estimator is not yet validated. A coverage check in the working simulation grid failed at the last snapshot. Until it is repaired, I will make no live-data claims.
  • A dependence-blind method may reach the same decision quality at lower audit cost. If it does, the certificate is not needed as an engineering requirement. I will compare against Fisher-z sizing, binomial bounds, an independence assumption, and Dawid-Skene style learners.
  • Open-weight verifiers are easier to control than vendor models, but their lineage must be documented. If I cannot document a family’s lineage, I will not count it as independent.
  • The threshold may be out of reach. If estimation is infeasible even at 1,500 adjudicated episodes, that negative result is itself a finding, and I will report it as one.

I welcome critique of the composition claim, especially the stratification step and the budget arithmetic.
